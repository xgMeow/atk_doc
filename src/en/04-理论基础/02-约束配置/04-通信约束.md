# Communications Constraints

This chapter gives the calculation models, formulas, and symbol definitions of the link metrics in Communications Constraints.

## 1 Overview and Notation

Communications Constraints determine whether the link between a transmitter and a receiver satisfies the requirements at a given time, according to link metrics. When only the minimum is enabled, the metric $x$ must satisfy $x\ge x_{\min}$; when only the maximum is enabled, $x\le x_{\max}$; when both are enabled:

$$
x_{\min}\le x\le x_{\max}
$$

When Reverse Selection is enabled, the result is inverted, that is, accesses that satisfy the range above are excluded.

The main symbols used in this chapter are: $f$ operating frequency, $c$ speed of light, $R$ transmitter-receiver distance, $P_t$ transmit power, $G_t$ transmit antenna gain, $G_r$ receive antenna gain, $L_{tf}$ antenna feed loss, $L_S$ free-space loss, $L_{other}$ other transmission losses such as atmospheric and polarization losses, $T_n$ equivalent noise temperature of the receiving system, and $K$ Boltzmann constant.

## 2 Operating Frequency

The operating frequency $f$ is the reference quantity of link calculation and takes part in the calculation of antenna gain, free-space loss, and Doppler shift; the corresponding wavelength is:

$$
\lambda=\frac{c}{f}
$$

The 【Frequency】 constraint limits the range of the link operating frequency $f$.

## 3 Link EIRP

The equivalent isotropically radiated power (EIRP) at the transmitting end is:

$$
EIRP=P_t+G_t-L_{tf}
$$

in dBW, where $P_t$ is the transmit power (dBW), $G_t$ is the transmit antenna gain (dBi), and $L_{tf}$ is the antenna feed loss (dB).

## 4 Free-Space Loss

The free-space loss is:

$$
L_S=10\times\log_{10}\left[\left(\frac{4\pi Rf}{c}\right)^2\right]=-147.56+20\times\log_{10}(R)+20\times\log_{10}(f)
$$

in dB, where $R$ is the transmitter-receiver distance (m), $f$ is the operating frequency (Hz), and $c$ is the speed of light.

## 5 Antenna Gain

For a parabolic antenna, the gain $G$ can be expressed as:

$$
G=10\times\log_{10}\left[\left(\frac{\pi Df}{c}\right)^2\eta\right]
$$

in dBi, where $D$ is the antenna diameter (m), $f$ is the operating frequency (MHz), and $\eta$ is the antenna efficiency.

## 6 Rcvd Isotropic Power

The received signal power $P_r$ can be expressed as:

$$
P_r=EIRP-L_S-L_{other}+G_r
$$

where $L_{other}$ represents other transmission losses of the link such as atmospheric and polarization losses, and $G_r$ is the receiving antenna gain.

It can also be expressed as:

$$
P_r=W_EA_r\eta
$$

where an antenna with aperture area $A_r$ and receiving efficiency $\eta$ receives the flux density $W_E$, which gives the received signal power.

The 【Rcvd Isotropic Power】 constraint limits the range of the desired received isotropic power $P_r$ at the input of the receiving antenna.

## 7 Flux Density

Assume a directional transmitting antenna with gain $G_t$ and transmit power $P_t$ (at the output of the transmitter power amplifier). At a distance $R$ from it, the flux density is:

$$
W_E=\frac{P_tG_t}{4\pi R^2}
$$

which is the power flux density at distance $R$ from the transmitting antenna (W/m²). The power flux density can also be calculated as:

$$
W_E=\Phi=RIP-20\times\log_{10}(c/f)+10\times\log_{10}(4\pi)
$$

where $f$ is the receiving frequency, $c$ is the speed of light, and $RIP$ is the equivalent radiated power, $RIP=EIRP-L_{pop}$, in which $L_{pop}$ is the channel propagation loss.

## 8 Doppler Shift

The Doppler shift is caused by the relative motion of the transmitting and receiving parties and can be expressed as:

$$
f_{out}=\frac{2v}{c}f_{in}
$$

where $f_{in}$ is the input frequency, $c$ is the speed of light, and $v$ is the relative velocity between the receiver and the transmitter.

## 9 System Noise Temperature

The equivalent noise temperature of the receiving system consists of external noise and internal noise.

### 9.1 External Noise Temperature of the Receiving System

The external noise temperature $T_a$ of the receiving system is the noise temperature generated before the input of the antenna feed, mainly including the sky background noise temperature $T_{sk}$, the atmospheric noise temperature $T_{at}$, and the antenna sidelobe noise temperature $T_{sl}$. The calculation formula for a ground receiving antenna is:

$$
T_a=\frac{T_{sk}}{L_a}+T_{at}+T_{sl}
$$

$$
T_{sl}=T_{sl1}+T_{sl2}+T_{sl3}
$$

All quantities in the formulas are in K.

The sky background noise temperature $T_{sk}$ mainly comes from galactic radiation noise entering through the antenna main lobe; its equivalent noise temperature $T_g$ can be estimated by the following three formulas ($f$ is the operating frequency in GHz, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength in m):

$$
T_{gmax}=40\times f^{2.31}
$$

$$
T_{gmin}=18.2\times f^{2.58}
$$

$$
T_{gava}=290\times\lambda^2
$$

$$
T_{sk}=T_g
$$

The atmospheric noise temperature depends on the atmospheric loss $L_a$ and can be estimated as:

$$
T_{at}=\left(1-\frac{1}{L_a}\right)\times T_0
$$

where $T_0$ is the atmospheric temperature, generally taken as 290 K.

The noise temperature caused by rain, clouds, fog, and snow is dominated by rain, and its magnitude depends on the rain loss $L_{ar}$:

$$
T_{ar}=\left(1-\frac{1}{L_{ar}}\right)\times T_0
$$

where $T_0$ is the atmospheric temperature of the rain, generally taken as 290 K; the noise caused by rain is usually considered in the system margin $M$.

The antenna sidelobe noise temperature $T_{sl}$ includes three sources: the ground noise temperature $T_{sl1}$, the sidelobe atmospheric noise temperature $T_{sl2}$, and the solar radiation noise temperature $T_{sl3}$. This part of the noise can be neglected when estimating the noise temperature of a spaceborne antenna. The ground noise temperature is mainly produced by terrestrial thermal radiation entering the antenna through the sidelobes, and its value is generally between 3 K and 20 K:

$$
T_{sl1}=\frac{2\pi}{4\pi}\times T_0\times g_{sl}
$$

where $T_0$ is the ground temperature, generally taken as 290 K, and $g_{sl}=0.1$, which means that, for a general antenna, all sidelobes except the main lobe can be roughly approximated as a single omnidirectional lobe with a gain of −10 dB.

The sidelobe atmospheric noise temperature is:

$$
T_{sl2}=0.5\times T_{at}\times g_{sl}
$$

where $T_{at}$ is the atmospheric noise temperature and $g_{sl}=0.1$.

For the solar radiation noise temperature, the apparent noise temperature $T_{\theta}$ of the quiet Sun is:

$$
T_{\theta}=\frac{675\times 290}{f}
$$

where $f$ is the operating frequency. When the Sun is highly active:

$$
T_{sl3}=\frac{\Omega_{\theta}}{4\pi}\times 10\times T_{\theta}\times g_{sl}
$$

where $\Omega_{\theta}=0.7\times 10^{-4}$ unit solid angle, and $g_{sl}=0.1$.

### 9.2 Internal Noise Temperature of the Receiving System

The noise temperature of the feed loss of the receiving system, referred to the feed output (receiver output), is:

$$
T_{rf}=\left(1-\frac{1}{L_{rf}}\right)\times T_0
$$

and referred to the feed input, it is:

$$
T_{rf}=(L_{rf}-1)\times T_0
$$

where $T_0$ is the physical temperature of the feed, generally 290 K on the ground and about 300 K on board, and $L_{rf}$ is the feed loss of the receiving system.

The equivalent noise temperature $T_r$ of the receiver, with the noise temperatures of all its parts referred to the receiver output, is given in the technical documents or derived from the noise figure $F_r$:

$$
F_r=1+\frac{T_r}{T_0}
$$

$$
T_r=(F_r-1)\times T_0
$$

where $T_0$ is the reference temperature of the noise source, generally 290 K.

The internal noise temperature of the receiving system is:

$$
T_i=T_{rf}+T_r
$$

where $T_{rf}$ is the feed loss noise temperature of the receiving system and $T_r$ is the receiver noise temperature.

### 9.3 Total Noise Temperature of the Receiving System

The total noise temperature of the receiving system is:

$$
T_n=\frac{T_a}{L_{rf}}+T_i
$$

where $T_a$ is the external noise temperature of the receiving system, $T_i$ is the internal noise temperature of the receiving system, and $L_{rf}$ is the feed loss of the receiving system; $T_a/L_{rf}$ represents the feed loss experienced by the external noise up to the receiver input.

## 10 G/T

The antenna performance quality factor (G/T) is the ratio of the receiving antenna gain to the equivalent noise temperature of the receiving system:

$$
G/T=G_r/T_n
$$

In decibels it is expressed as $G/T=G_r-T_n$, in dB/K, where $G_r$ is the receiving antenna gain and $T_n$ is the equivalent noise temperature of the receiving system at an elevation angle of 5° (including external and internal noise).

## 11 Noise Power Spectral Density

If the total equivalent noise temperature of the receiving system is $T_n$, already referred to the input of the receiver, then the total noise power $N$ within the receiver operating bandwidth $2B$ (Hz) is:

$$
N=KT_n\cdot 2B
$$

where $K$ is the Boltzmann constant:

$$
K=1.38\times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{W\cdot s/K}
$$

The noise power spectral density $N_0$ represents the noise power per unit bandwidth:

$$
N_0=\frac{N}{2B}=KT_n
$$

in W/Hz. Expressed in decibels:

$$
N_0=K+T_n=-228.6+T_n
$$

in dBHz.

## 12 Carrier-to-Noise Density Ratio

The received carrier-to-noise density ratio $C/N_0$ is:

$$
C/N_0=\frac{P_r}{N_0}=EIRP-L_S-L_{other}+G/T
$$

where $EIRP$ is the equivalent isotropically radiated power of the transmission, in dBW; $L_S$ is the free-space loss; $L_{other}$ is the other losses; and $G/T$ is the ratio of antenna gain to noise temperature.

## 13 Power at Receiver Input

The power at the receiver input is the value of the received signal power $P_r$ at the input of the receiver front-end amplifier, and its calculation model is the same as that in Section 6:

$$
P_r=EIRP-L_S-L_{other}+G_r
$$

where $EIRP$ is the link equivalent isotropically radiated power, $L_S$ is the free-space loss, $L_{other}$ is the other transmission losses such as atmospheric and polarization losses, and $G_r$ is the receiving antenna gain.

## 14 Carrier-to-Noise Ratio

The received signal-to-noise ratio $S/N$ is:

$$
S/N=\frac{P_r}{N_0}-2B
$$

where $2B$ is the receiver operating bandwidth (Hz).

## 15 Energy per Bit to Noise Density Ratio

Useful information is modulated onto an information subcarrier after coding, so the power of the information subcarrier is the sum of the energy of all symbols per unit time:

$$
S=E_b\times b_r
$$

where $S$ is the power of the information subcarrier, $E_b$ is the energy of a single symbol, and $b_r$ is the code rate (b/s). Therefore:

$$
S/N_0=\frac{E_b\times b_r}{N_0}
$$

Expressed in decibels, $S/N_0=E_b/N_0+b_r$, that is:

$$
E_b/N_0=S/N_0-b_r
$$

## 16 Bit Error Rate

The bit error rate $P_e$ is determined by the modulation scheme and $E_b/N_0$.

For BPSK modulation, the bit error rate is:

$$
P_e=Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right)
$$

The relation between the Q function and the complementary error function erfc is:

$$
Q(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
$$

$$
\mathrm{erfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\right)=1-\mathrm{erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\right)
$$

$$
\mathrm{erf}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi^{1/2}}\int_0^t e^{-x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

where $\mathrm{erf}(t)$ is the normal error function, whose values can be obtained from the normal error function table, and $\mathrm{erfc}(t)=1-\mathrm{erf}(t)$ is the complementary normal error function.

For MPSK modulation, the bit error rate is:

$$
P_e=2Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{4E_b}{N_0}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2M}\right)\right)
$$

where $M$ is the modulation order, such as 4, 8, or 16.

## 17 Link Margin

Comparing $E_b/N_0$ with the $E_b/N_0$ threshold, their difference is the link margin:

$$
M=E_b/N_0-\left(E_b/N_0\right)_{\mathrm{th}}
$$

in dB. A positive link margin means that the link meets the minimum communications requirement with margin to spare, and the link status is judged accordingly.

## 18 Polarization Relative Angle

Link calculation uses the Poincaré sphere to represent the polarization state. To calculate the loss caused by polarization mismatch, the angle between the reference directions of the transmitting and receiving ends perpendicular to the boresight plane is computed; this angle is used to rotate the Poincaré sphere representation of the transmitter in the receiver coordinate system. The resulting loss is the square of the cosine of that angle, that is, the loss depends on twice the angle between the two polarization states. When a polarization mode is selected in the link calculation, the polarization-related components of the antenna and system noise temperature ($T_{sun}$, $T_{earth}$, $T_{rain}$, $T_{atmos}$, $T_{cosmic}$, $T_{other}$) are halved, which affects the calculation of the equivalent noise temperature $T_{equivalent}$.

For horizontal and vertical polarization, the polarization loss is:

$$
L=20\log_{10}\left(\cos\theta\right)
$$

in dB, where $\theta$ is the polarization angle between the receiving and transmitting antennas. For right-hand circular polarization:

$$
L=20\log_{10}\left(\left|\cos\theta\right|\right)
$$

For left-hand circular polarization:

$$
L_p=-10\lg\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\frac{\pm 4X_rX_t+(1-X_t^2)(1-X_r^2)\cos 2\alpha}{(1+X_t^2)(1+X_r^2)}\right]
$$

in dB. The ratio of the electric field strength along the major axis to that along the minor axis of the polarization ellipse is called the axial ratio, which represents the degree of circularity error of the circular polarization and is denoted by $X_t$ and $X_r$, where $X_t$ is the axial ratio of the incident wave and $X_r$ is the axial ratio of the receiving antenna; the angle between the axis directions of the incident wave polarization ellipse and the receiving antenna polarization ellipse is $\alpha$. The sign of $\pm 4X_rX_t$ depends on whether the polarization rotation direction of the incident wave is the same as that of the receiving antenna: "+" if they are the same and "−" if they are opposite. When the incident wave and the receiving antenna have the same circular polarization, $L_p=1$, that is, 0 dB; when a circularly polarized antenna receives a linearly polarized wave, $L_p=1/2$, that is, a loss of 3 dB.

The 【Polarization Relative Angle】 constraint limits the range of the relative angle $\theta$ between the polarization plane of the transmitting antenna and the polarization plane of the receiving antenna.
