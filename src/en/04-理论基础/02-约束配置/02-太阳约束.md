# Sun Constraints

## 1. Objects and Geometric Quantities

Sun constraints involve the geometric relationships among the constrained object, the associated object, and the Sun, the Moon, or other specified celestial bodies. Let A be the constrained object, B the associated object, and C the central body of A. The position vectors of the objects, expressed in the same reference system and evaluated at the same geometric computation time, are denoted by $\boldsymbol r_A$, $\boldsymbol r_B$, $\boldsymbol r_C$, $\boldsymbol r_\odot$, and $\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm M}$, respectively.

Define:

$$
\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol r_A-\boldsymbol r_C,
\qquad
\boldsymbol l=\boldsymbol r_B-\boldsymbol r_A,
$$

$$
\boldsymbol s=\boldsymbol r_\odot-\boldsymbol r_A,
\qquad
\boldsymbol m=\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm M}-\boldsymbol r_A.
$$

Here, $\boldsymbol r$ is the radial vector from the central body to A, $\boldsymbol l$ is the line-of-sight vector from A to B, and $\boldsymbol s$ and $\boldsymbol m$ are the vectors from A toward the Sun and the Moon, respectively.

The following trigonometric expressions use radians. Use consistent units when comparing the calculated angles with values entered in the interface. The conversion from radians to degrees is:

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{deg}}=\frac{180}{\pi}\theta_{\mathrm{rad}}.
$$

This chapter first presents a geometric model in which all quantities are evaluated at the same time. Access calculations that apply light-time corrections must use direction vectors based on the corresponding time convention. Corrected directions must not be combined directly with uncorrected positions.

## 2. Sun and Lunar Elevation Angles

### 2.1 Reference Plane and Angle Definitions

This section uses a reference plane defined by the radial direction. A reference horizontal plane perpendicular to $\boldsymbol r$ is established at A. Its outward unit normal vector is:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol n}_r=\frac{\boldsymbol r}{\|\boldsymbol r\|}.
$$

For a nonzero direction vector $\boldsymbol d$ from A toward a celestial body, the elevation angle of that body relative to the plane is:

$$
E=\arcsin\left(
\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol n}_r\cdot\boldsymbol d}{\|\boldsymbol d\|}
\right).
$$

Substituting the Sun and Moon directions, respectively, gives:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Sun}}
=\frac{\pi}{2}-\operatorname{atan2}
\left(\|\boldsymbol r\times\boldsymbol s\|,\boldsymbol r\cdot\boldsymbol s\right),
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{Moon}}
=\frac{\pi}{2}-\operatorname{atan2}
\left(\|\boldsymbol r\times\boldsymbol m\|,\boldsymbol r\cdot\boldsymbol m\right).
$$

These two equations give the Sun and lunar elevation angles relative to the reference plane defined by the radial direction. In both cases, the angle vertex is A, not the associated object B.

### 2.2 Constraint Criteria

The theoretical range of an elevation angle is $[-90^\circ,90^\circ]$. A positive value indicates that the body is above the reference horizontal plane; a negative value indicates that it is below the plane; and zero indicates that the body direction lies in the plane.

When only Min is enabled, the requirement is $E\geq E_{\min}$. When only Max is enabled, the requirement is $E\leq E_{\max}$. When both are enabled, the requirement is:

$$
E_{\min}\leq E\leq E_{\max}.
$$

> **Applicability Note**: The plane above is defined by the radial direction from the central body. It is not the apparent horizon formed by the body limb as seen from a space-based object. For an object at a nonzero altitude, the Sun being below this reference horizontal plane does not necessarily mean that the central body obstructs the Sun.

## 3. Sun Gnd Elev Angle

The Sun Gnd Elev Angle is measured relative to the local horizontal plane of the reference ground object. Let G be this ground object, with position $\boldsymbol r_G$. Its vector toward the Sun is:

$$
\boldsymbol s_G=\boldsymbol r_\odot-\boldsymbol r_G.
$$

Here, G denotes the ground reference object actually used by this constraint. It is not assumed in advance to be either the object initiating the access calculation or the associated object. The mapping between object types and reference objects must remain consistent with the rules ATK uses when invoking the constraint. The publicly available ATK documentation defines the local Sun elevation angle in the local coordinate system of a ground object, such as a Facility.

Let $\hat{\boldsymbol n}_G$ be the unit vector in the local zenith direction. Then:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Local}}
=\arcsin\left(
\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol n}_G\cdot\boldsymbol s_G}{\|\boldsymbol s_G\|}
\right).
$$

If the LH coordinate convention has its $OZ$ axis pointing locally downward, and the Sun vector is transformed into this coordinate system:

$$
\boldsymbol s_{G,\mathrm{LH}}
=\begin{bmatrix}s_{x,\mathrm{LH}}&s_{y,\mathrm{LH}}&s_{z,\mathrm{LH}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T},
$$

the component form is:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Local}}
=\arcsin\left(-\frac{s_{z,\mathrm{LH}}}{\|\boldsymbol s_G\|}\right)
=\operatorname{atan2}\left(
-s_{z,\mathrm{LH}},
\sqrt{s_{x,\mathrm{LH}}^2+s_{y,\mathrm{LH}}^2}
\right).
$$

The minus sign results from the downward-axis convention of the local coordinate system. If the vertical axis points upward, the corresponding component formula has no minus sign. The physical definition, however, is always “positive above the horizontal plane.” The STK description of LH coordinates and the ATK description of North-East-Down coordinates both require a clear distinction between the upward and downward directions.

**Sun Elevation Angle and Sun Gnd Elev Angle differ in the definitions of their reference points and reference planes.** Section 2 defines the reference plane using the radial direction from the central body, whereas this section uses the local horizontal plane of a ground object. In an ellipsoidal model, the local zenith is usually taken to be the surface normal, which need not coincide with the radial direction from the central body. The two expressions are equivalent only when both the reference point and the reference plane are the same.

The lower- and upper-limit criteria for Sun Gnd Elev Angle are the same as those in Section 2.2.

## 4. Line Of Sight Exclusion Angles

### 4.1 General Angle Model

An exclusion angle constraint compares the actual angle between a reference direction and the direction to a specified celestial body. Let $\boldsymbol a$ be a nonzero vector in the reference direction, and let $\boldsymbol d$ be a nonzero vector from the constrained object toward the body. Then:

$$
\alpha=\arccos\left(
\frac{\boldsymbol a\cdot\boldsymbol d}
{\|\boldsymbol a\|\,\|\boldsymbol d\|}
\right).
$$

Here, $\alpha$ is the actual geometric angle, whereas $\alpha_{\min}$ entered in the interface is the minimum allowable angle. The two quantities must not be confused. The usual exclusion mode requires:

$$
\alpha\geq\alpha_{\min}.
$$

When $\alpha<\alpha_{\min}$, the reference direction is too close to the body direction, and the angular condition is not satisfied.

Geometrically, the set of excluded directions forms a cone with its vertex at the constrained object, its axis along the body direction, and its half-angle equal to $\alpha_{\min}$. The reference direction is tested to determine whether it lies within this cone; it does not define the center of another cone.

### 4.2 Line Of Sight Exclusion Angles

Line-of-sight exclusion uses the vector $\boldsymbol l$ from A to B as the reference:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{LOS,Sun}}
=\arccos\left(
\frac{\boldsymbol l\cdot\boldsymbol s}{\|\boldsymbol l\|\,\|\boldsymbol s\|}
\right),
\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{LOS,Moon}}
=\arccos\left(
\frac{\boldsymbol l\cdot\boldsymbol m}{\|\boldsymbol l\|\,\|\boldsymbol m\|}
\right).
$$

Compare the actual angles with the solar and lunar exclusion thresholds, respectively. This is consistent with the ATK description of line-of-sight exclusion angles.

### 4.3 Exclusion Angles for Specified Celestial Bodies

For a specified celestial body K, let $\boldsymbol d_K=\boldsymbol r_K-\boldsymbol r_A$. When the access line of sight is used as the reference:

$$
\alpha_K=\arccos\left(
\frac{\boldsymbol l\cdot\boldsymbol d_K}
{\|\boldsymbol l\|\,\|\boldsymbol d_K\|}
\right).
$$

In the usual exclusion mode, the criterion is $\alpha_K\geq\alpha_{K,\min}$. This definition is a general model for the angle between directions. It does not include a correction for the apparent angular radius of the body, nor does it specify the meaning of the 【Exclude】 column in the ATK interface.

## 5. Additional Central Body Obstruction

A body obstruction test determines whether the path between the access objects passes through the physical body. The line segment connecting A and B is:

$$
\boldsymbol x(\lambda)=\boldsymbol r_A+\lambda(\boldsymbol r_B-\boldsymbol r_A),
\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
$$

Let $\operatorname{int}(\Omega_K)$ denote the interior of the selected celestial body K. If there exists $0<\lambda<1$ such that:

$$
\boldsymbol x(\lambda)\in\operatorname{int}(\Omega_K),
$$

then the access path passes through the interior of the body, and obstruction occurs.

For example, in a spherical model used to illustrate the principle, the body center is $\boldsymbol r_K$ and its radius is $R_K$. The condition above can be written as the existence of $0<\lambda<1$ satisfying:

$$
\|\boldsymbol x(\lambda)-\boldsymbol r_K\|<R_K.
$$

The spherical expression only illustrates the geometric principle; it does not confirm that the ATK computational core uses a spherical model. The actual body shape, treatment of tangency, and numerical tolerances are determined by the software implementation.

The test must consider the finite line segment between A and B. Access must not be classified as obstructed merely because the infinitely extended line intersects a body. When multiple additional bodies are selected, the access path must be unobstructed by every selected body.

**Obstruction constraints and exclusion angle constraints are not equivalent.** The former determine whether a body obstructs the access path, whereas the latter determine whether the access direction is too close to the body direction. An unobstructed access path can still fail an exclusion angle requirement.
