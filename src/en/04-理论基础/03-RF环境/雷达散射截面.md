# Radar Cross Section

## 1. Physical Meaning of the Radar Cross Section

The radar cross section (RCS) describes the ability of a target to scatter energy back toward the radar receiving direction when illuminated by an electromagnetic wave, and is usually denoted by:

$$
\sigma
$$

The dimension of RCS is area, but it is not the true geometric area of the target. It can be understood as an "equivalent scattering area": if an ideal scatterer can produce a radar echo of the same strength as the real target, then the equivalent area corresponding to this ideal scatterer can be used to represent the RCS of the real target.

Under far-field conditions, the RCS can be defined using the incident power density and the scattered power density. Let:

- $S_i$ be the incident power density at the target position;
- $S_s(R)$ be the scattered power density at a distance $R$ from the target in the specified scattering direction;

then:

$$
\sigma
=
\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}
4\pi R^2
\frac{S_s(R)}{S_i}.
$$

Therefore, a larger $\sigma$ means a stronger ability of the target to scatter in the specified direction.

For a monostatic radar, the transmitter and the receiver are at the same position, and the backscattering RCS of the target returning to the radar along the incident direction is usually of interest.

> **Note**: RCS is an electromagnetic scattering quantity. A larger target size does not necessarily mean a larger RCS; factors such as target shape, attitude, material, frequency, and polarization all affect the scattering result.

## 2. Units of RCS and dBsm

The linear unit of RCS is usually the square meter:

$$
\mathrm{m^2}.
$$

In engineering, dBsm (decibels relative to one square meter) is commonly used:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{dBsm}}
=
10\log_{10}
\left(
\frac{\sigma}{1\ \mathrm{m^2}}
\right).
$$

Converting back to a linear area:

$$
\sigma
=
10^{\sigma_{\mathrm{dBsm}}/10}\ \mathrm{m^2}.
$$

For example:

| Linear RCS | dBsm |
|---:|---:|
| $0.01\ \mathrm{m^2}$ | $-20\ \mathrm{dBsm}$ |
| $0.1\ \mathrm{m^2}$ | $-10\ \mathrm{dBsm}$ |
| $1\ \mathrm{m^2}$ | $0\ \mathrm{dBsm}$ |
| $10\ \mathrm{m^2}$ | $10\ \mathrm{dBsm}$ |
| $100\ \mathrm{m^2}$ | $20\ \mathrm{dBsm}$ |

A constant RCS is used in dBsm; internally it can be equivalently converted to square meters for calculation.

## 3. Role of RCS in the Radar Equation

For an ideal monostatic radar, after neglecting other details, the received power of the target echo can be expressed as:

$$
P_r
=
\frac{
P_tG_tG_r\lambda^2\sigma
}{
(4\pi)^3R^4L
},
$$

where:

- $P_t$ is the transmit power;
- $G_t$ is the antenna gain in the transmit direction;
- $G_r$ is the antenna gain in the receive direction;
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength;
- $\sigma$ is the target RCS;
- $R$ is the distance from the radar to the target;
- $L$ is the composite factor of propagation, system, and other losses.

If the same antenna is used for both transmission and reception, and the gains in the transmit and receive directions are the same, this can be written as:

$$
P_r
=
\frac{
P_tG^2\lambda^2\sigma
}{
(4\pi)^3R^4L
}.
$$

It can be seen that:

$$
P_r\propto\sigma,
$$

and at the same time:

$$
P_r\propto\frac{1}{R^4}.
$$

Therefore, with other conditions unchanged, a larger RCS means a stronger echo power; and an increase in the target distance causes the echo to decay rapidly.

This is also the reason why the RCS directly affects the radar signal-to-noise ratio, the detection probability, and the maximum detection range.

For a general bistatic radar, the transmitting radar and the receiving radar are at different positions, and the propagation term in the radar equation is extended to the transmit distance $R_t$ and the receive distance $R_r$:

$$
P_r
=
\frac{
P_tG_tG_r\lambda^2\sigma_b
}{
(4\pi)^3R_t^2R_r^2L
},
$$

where $\sigma_b$ is the bistatic RCS corresponding to the incident direction and the scattering direction.

## 4. Frequency, Aspect, and Polarization Dependence of RCS

The RCS of a real target is generally not a fixed constant, and can be written as:

$$
\sigma
=
\sigma
\left(
f,\Omega_i,\Omega_s,\mathrm{pol},\mathrm{geometry},\mathrm{material}
\right),
$$

where:

- $f$ is the radar frequency;
- $\Omega_i$ is the incident direction;
- $\Omega_s$ is the scattering/receiving direction;
- $\mathrm{pol}$ denotes the polarization state of the electromagnetic wave;
- $\mathrm{geometry}$ denotes the target geometry and attitude;
- $\mathrm{material}$ denotes the electromagnetic properties of the target material.

In a monostatic radar, the incident and receiving directions are determined by the same radar–target geometry, so the most common form is:

$$
\sigma
=
\sigma(f,\rho,\theta).
$$

This shows that the RCS can vary with both frequency and target aspect at the same time.

### 4.1 Frequency Dependence

The wavelength of the electromagnetic wave is:

$$
\lambda=\frac{c}{f}.
$$

When the frequency changes, the relative relationship between the target size and the wavelength also changes, and the scattering mechanism may change accordingly. Therefore, the same target can have different RCS values in different radar bands.

ATK uses a band table to divide the overall range of about 3 MHz to 300 GHz into one or more intervals:

$$
B_i
=
[f_{\min,i},f_{\max,i}].
$$

When the radar computation frequency $f$ falls into the $i$-th band:

$$
f\in B_i,
$$

the software uses the RCS computation type and Swerling model defined for that band.

This band structure allows the same object to use different RCS data for different radar frequencies.

### 4.2 Aspect-Dependent RCS

For non-spherical targets such as aircraft, missiles, satellites, and ships, the target geometry seen by the radar differs when illuminated from different directions, and the RCS may differ greatly.

Therefore, more generally, it can be expressed as:

$$
\sigma=\sigma(\rho,\theta),
$$

or, taking frequency into account:

$$
\sigma=\sigma(f,\rho,\theta).
$$

In the RCS direction definition, Rho and Theta can be used within the target body coordinate system to describe the aspect:

- **Rho**: the total angle between the target RCS reference axis and the radar line-of-sight vector;
- **Theta**: the azimuth of the radar line of sight in the plane perpendicular to the reference axis.

The RCS reference axis can usually be defined as the $X$ axis or the $Z$ axis of the target body coordinate system.

Therefore, an external RCS file essentially provides one or more "scattering pattern charts":

$$
(\rho,\theta)
\longrightarrow
\sigma.
$$

Its form is similar to that of an antenna pattern, but its physical meaning is different:

$$
\text{Antenna pattern: }
(\theta,\phi)\rightarrow G,
$$

$$
\text{RCS pattern: }
(\rho,\theta)\rightarrow\sigma.
$$

The former describes the transmit/receive capability of the antenna in different directions, while the latter describes the scattering capability of the target when illuminated from different aspects.

## 5. Constant RCS Model

When 【Computation Type】 is set to 【Constant】 in ATK, a fixed reference RCS is used over the entire corresponding band:

$$
\sigma(f,\rho,\theta)
\approx
\sigma_0.
$$

The constant model ignores:

- Changes in target attitude;
- Changes in aspect;
- Target geometric details;
- Subtle frequency variations within the band;

It is therefore suitable for:

- Preliminary analysis when detailed target scattering data are unavailable;
- Uniform performance assumptions at the system design stage;
- Scenarios where only the equivalent target strength is of interest and attitude effects need not be studied.

In the Swerling 0 case:

$$
\sigma(t)=\sigma_0,
$$

that is, the RCS does not fluctuate randomly.

If Swerling I to IV is used, then, according to the standard Swerling models, this reference RCS is taken as the mean/nominal level of the target RCS, and the corresponding statistical fluctuation is generated.

## 6. External RCS File Model

When 【Computation Type】 is set to 【External File】, ATK reads the target RCS data from a file to describe aspect-dependent scattering characteristics that are more realistic than the constant model.

### 6.1 Basic Data Forms

The most common real-valued RCS file is given by aspect:

$$
(\rho,\theta,\sigma).
$$

For axisymmetric targets, if the RCS depends only on Rho, this can be simplified to:

$$
(\rho,\sigma).
$$

For more complex models, a complex scattering matrix can also be used to describe scattering in different polarization channels:

$$
\mathbf S_{\mathrm{RCS}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
S_{11} & S_{12}\\
S_{21} & S_{22}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

where:

- $S_{11}$: the co-polarization channel;
- $S_{12}$: the cross-polarization component from the co-polarization to the orthogonal polarization;
- $S_{21}$: the cross-polarization component from the orthogonal polarization to the co-polarization;
- $S_{22}$: the orthogonal polarization channel.

Whether real-valued RCS, a complex scattering matrix, monostatic data, or bistatic data is used depends on the external file format and the parsing methods supported by the current ATK version.

### 6.2 Numerical Scale in RCS Files

RCS files compatible with STK can use:

- dBsm;
- Linear square meters $\mathrm{m^2}$.

If the file declares the use of a linear RCS scale, the data are expressed in square meters; otherwise, conventional RCS files are usually expressed in dBsm.

### 6.3 External Files and Target Attitude

When aspect-dependent external RCS is used, the definition of the target body coordinate system is very important.

The software needs to transform:

$$
\text{radar–target geometric direction}
$$

into:

$$
\text{target body coordinate system}
$$

to obtain the current $\rho$ and $\theta$, and then look up or interpolate from the external data to obtain:

$$
\sigma(\rho,\theta).
$$

Therefore, even if the RCS file itself is correct, an incorrect definition of the target attitude, coordinate axes, or reference axis may still yield an incorrect RCS.

## 7. Swerling RCS Fluctuation Models

### 7.1 Why Fluctuation Models Are Needed

A real target usually consists of multiple scattering centers. As the target attitude, radar aspect, target micro-motion, and frequency change slightly, the phase relationships of the echoes from the various scattering centers change, causing the total echo to fluctuate randomly.

Therefore, even if the target has a mean RCS:

$$
\bar{\sigma},
$$

the instantaneous RCS may still appear as:

$$
\sigma(t)\neq\bar{\sigma}.
$$

The Swerling models describe this target RCS fluctuation through statistical distributions and temporal correlation.

ATK supports:

$$
\boxed{
0,\ \mathrm{I},\ \mathrm{II},\ \mathrm{III},\ \mathrm{IV}
}
$$

five Swerling settings.

### 7.2 Swerling 0

Swerling 0 means that random RCS fluctuation is not considered:

$$
\sigma(t)=\bar{\sigma}.
$$

In this case the target RCS is deterministic.

If the computation type is 【Constant】, the configured constant RCS is used directly; if the computation type is 【External File】, the deterministic RCS obtained at the current frequency and aspect is used.

### 7.3 Swerling I and II

Swerling I and Swerling II use the same RCS probability distribution but have different temporal correlations.

Their RCS follows an exponential distribution:

$$
p(\sigma)
=
\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}}
\right),
\qquad
\sigma\geq0.
$$

where:

$$
E[\sigma]=\bar{\sigma}.
$$

This class of models is usually used to describe targets composed of multiple scattering centers of approximately comparable strength with no obvious dominant scattering center.

The difference between the two is:

- **Swerling I**: the RCS remains correlated during one scan/dwell and changes again between scans;
- **Swerling II**: the RCS fluctuates faster and can change from pulse to pulse.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{I}:\ \text{slow fluctuation}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{II}:\ \text{fast fluctuation}}
$$

### 7.4 Swerling III and IV

Swerling III and IV are applicable to targets that have one obvious dominant scattering center together with several smaller scattering centers.

The RCS follows a chi-square distribution with four degrees of freedom:

$$
p(\sigma)
=
\frac{4\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}^{\,2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{2\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}}
\right),
\qquad
\sigma\geq0.
$$

Its mean is still:

$$
E[\sigma]=\bar{\sigma}.
$$

The difference between the two is again the fluctuation time scale:

- **Swerling III**: remains correlated within one scan/dwell and changes between scans;
- **Swerling IV**: can change from pulse to pulse.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{III}:\ \text{dominant scattering center + slow fluctuation}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{IV}:\ \text{dominant scattering center + fast fluctuation}}
$$

### 7.5 Comparison of the Five Models

| Swerling Model | RCS Probability Model | Temporal Correlation | Typical Scattering Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Deterministic value | No fluctuation | Fixed RCS |
| I | Exponential distribution | Varies between scans | Multiple scattering centers of approximately equal strength |
| II | Exponential distribution | Varies between pulses | Multiple scattering centers of approximately equal strength |
| III | Chi-square distribution with 4 degrees of freedom | Varies between scans | One dominant scattering center + multiple smaller scattering centers |
| IV | Chi-square distribution with 4 degrees of freedom | Varies between pulses | One dominant scattering center + multiple smaller scattering centers |

Note that:

> The difference between Swerling I and II lies not in the probability distribution but in the time scale on which the RCS is updated; the same is true for Swerling III and IV.

## 8. RCS, Swerling Models, and Detection Probability

Whether a radar can detect a target depends not only on the mean RCS, but also on the received signal-to-noise ratio, the pulse integration, and the target fluctuation model.

For a search/track radar, the detection probability can be summarized as:

$$
P_D
=
F
\left(
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{pulse}},
N,
P_{FA},
\text{Swerling Case}
\right),
$$

where:

- $P_D$ is the detection probability;
- $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{pulse}}$ is the single-pulse signal-to-noise ratio;
- $N$ is the number of pulses participating in the integration;
- $P_{FA}$ is the false alarm probability;
- Swerling Case is the type of target RCS fluctuation.

The RCS first affects the echo power through the radar equation:

$$
\sigma
\rightarrow
P_r,
$$

then affects the signal-to-noise ratio:

$$
P_r
\rightarrow
\mathrm{SNR},
$$

and finally, together with the pulse integration, the false alarm probability, and the Swerling fluctuation model, determines:

$$
\mathrm{SNR}
\rightarrow
P_D.
$$

Therefore, the RCS page is not merely a "target area setting", but the target scattering model in the radar detection performance calculation.

## 9. Computation Logic of RCS

The RCS computation process can be summarized as follows:

### 9.1 Select the Band According to the Radar Frequency

Let the current radar computation frequency be $f$.

Search the RCS band table for the band $B_i$ satisfying:

$$
f_{\min,i}
\leq
f
\leq
f_{\max,i}
$$

### 9.2 Read the RCS Computation Type of That Band

If the computation type is 【Constant】:

$$
\bar{\sigma}
=
\sigma_{0,i}.
$$

If the computation type is 【External File】, then, according to the current frequency, target attitude, and radar aspect, obtain from the external data:

$$
\bar{\sigma}
=
\sigma_i(f,\rho,\theta).
$$

### 9.3 Apply the Swerling Model

If:

$$
\text{Swerling}=0,
$$

then:

$$
\sigma=\bar{\sigma}.
$$

If:

$$
\text{Swerling}\in
\{\mathrm{I},\mathrm{II},\mathrm{III},\mathrm{IV}\},
$$

then the target RCS fluctuation is processed according to the corresponding statistical distribution and temporal correlation model.

### 9.4 Enter the Radar Echo Calculation

After the target RCS is obtained:

$$
\sigma
\rightarrow
P_r
\rightarrow
\mathrm{SNR}
\rightarrow
P_D.
$$

Therefore the complete logic can be summarized as:

$$
\boxed{
f
\rightarrow
\text{RCS band}
\rightarrow
\text{constant/external file}
\rightarrow
\bar{\sigma}
\rightarrow
\text{Swerling}
\rightarrow
\sigma
\rightarrow
P_r
\rightarrow
\mathrm{SNR}
\rightarrow
P_D
}
$$

## 10. Model Selection

If only one equivalent RCS of the target is known, and the main purpose is to verify the radar link or perform a preliminary performance analysis, the following can be used:

$$
\boxed{\text{constant RCS + Swerling 0}}
$$

If the mean RCS of the target is known but the random fluctuation of target scattering is to be taken into account, a constant RCS together with Swerling I to IV can be used.

If RCS data that vary with attitude or aspect are available, the following should be preferred:

$$
\boxed{\text{external RCS file}}
$$

and the Swerling model should be selected according to the actual fluctuation characteristics of the target.

For high-fidelity analysis, the RCS data can come from:

- Electromagnetic scattering simulation;
- Measured RCS data;
- Other validated radar scattering models.
